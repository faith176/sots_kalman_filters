\subsubsection{\textit{Typical SoTS coordination architectures underemphasize dynamic SoS properties}}

\textbf{Key observation: Most SoTS adopt coordination architectures that emphasize stable operation (RQ2), with limited attention to dynamic SoS properties such as adaptation and emergence (RQ4).}

The architectural analysis in \secref{sec:architecture} reveals a clear dominance of SoTS configurations with explicitly assigned coordination roles, with acknowledged (\xofyp{31}{80}) and directed (\xofyp{26}{80}) SoTS accounting for the majority of studies. Collaborative (\xofyp{19}{80}) and virtual (\xofyp{4}{80}) SoTS remain comparatively rare. Although many studies frame their systems as SoS, this distribution indicates that coordination logic is most often assigned to designated coordinating entities rather than emerging from interactions among autonomous constituents. This tendency is reinforced by the SoS dimension analysis (\secref{sec:dimensions}). While SoS properties such as distribution and independence are widely supported, SoS properties that arise through inter-constituent interaction, specifically reconfiguration (\xofyp{35}{80}) and evolution (\xofyp{30}{80}), are among the least frequently addressed. Emergence is absent or only partially considered in a substantial portion of the sample and, when discussed, is most often limited to weak or predictable forms (\secref{sec:emergence-type}). As a result, challenges related to changing goals and changing system membership remain underexplored.

\begin{recoframe}{Recommendation}
Explore SoTS architectures that rely on inter-constituent interaction to realize system coordination, specifically virtual and collaborative SoTS, and examine how collective behaviour differs from architectures that use assigned coordination roles.
\end{recoframe}

Shifting toward choreography-oriented SoTS means moving away from orchestration-oriented coordination, enabling participants to reason locally about their behavior in relation to others, with global behavior emerging from their interactions rather than from a central controller~\cite{peltz2003web}. We therefore recommend that future SoTS research explicitly investigate choreography-oriented SoTS using implementation approaches that support such forms of interaction \secref{sec:classification}.
\textbf{Multi-agent system (MAS)} models provide a proven mechanism for realizing choreography-oriented coordination, as they enable autonomous entities to pursue local goals, negotiate with peers, and adapt to changing conditions through message-based interaction, making them well suited for studying reconfiguration and recovery under uncertainty~\cite{leite2013systematic}. Recent SoTS work further demonstrates the practical feasibility of this approach through multi-agent DT architectures for distributed industrial control~\cite{jirsa2024use}. In parallel, \textbf{contract-based design} offers a principled way to constrain interaction without restricting constituent autonomy: assumption-guarantee contracts enable compositional reasoning about compatibility, substitution, and evolution~\cite{benveniste2012contracts}, while formal results show that global choreography correctness can be ensured through local contract conformance rather than centralized control~\cite{bravetti2007towards}.

\subsubsection{\textit{SoTS evaluation is predominantly success-oriented and assumes standard operating conditions}}

\textbf{Key observation: Existing SoTS evaluations primarily assess feasibility under standard operating conditions, with limited attention to failure modes or situations in which expected operating conditions do not hold (RQ5, RQ6).}

Although reliability (\xofyp{41}{80}) and security (\xofyp{19}{80}) are frequently mentioned, they are rarely modeled or evaluated explicitly. As shown in \secref{results-rq5} and \secref{results-rq6}, most studies rely on validation through prototyping or simulation, typically assuming stable communication, timely data availability, and correct constituent behavior. Targeted evaluation of adverse operating situations---such as delayed or missing updates, inconsistent state across DTs, or partial system unavailability---is uncommon. This success-oriented evaluation bias aligns with the dominant motivations of optimization and integration (\secref{sec:results-rq1}), but limits insight into how SoTS behave when coordination conditions change or break down. Such situations are especially relevant in large-scale, distributed SoS, where system structure, constituent membership, and goals may evolve over time.

\begin{recoframe}{Recommendation}
Evaluate SoTS beyond standard operating conditions to better assess reliability, security, and overall dependability in realistic settings.
\end{recoframe}

Future evaluations should explore failure scenarios and changing operating conditions. Prior work on SoS evaluation shows that interdependencies between constituent systems can give rise to cascading failures and hidden vulnerabilities that remain invisible under idealized assumptions \cite{eusgeld2011system}. In particular, the systematic analysis of ``what-if'' scenarios involving delayed information, partial system blindness, or component unavailability is essential for understanding failure propagation across interdependent systems. From a dependability perspective, system quality should be assessed based on the ability to sustain acceptable service levels before, during, and after disruptive events \cite{bukowski2016dependability}. Reporting how SoTS respond, recover, or reconfigure under such conditions would therefore strengthen dependability claims and provide more realistic evidence for deployment in operational environments.



\subsubsection{\textit{Cross-domain transferability of SoTS designs in socio-technical contexts}}

\textbf{Key observation: Most SoTS studies are conducted in industrial domains (RQ1) and primarily model constituent systems as fully autonomous physical DTs, which limits the transferability of SoTS designs to socio-technical settings (RQ2, RQ3).}

As reported in \secref{sec:results-rq1}, existing SoTS studies are concentrated in manufacturing (\xofyp{32}{80}). This domain is well suited to early SoTS research because it involves complex physical assets, well-defined performance objectives, and strong incentives for monitoring, optimization, and automation. This domain focus is reflected in how constituent systems are modeled. As shown in \secref{results-rq2} and \secref{results-rq3}, the majority of studies represent physical assets using fully autonomous DTs (\xofyp{66}{80}), while cyber-physical-human systems (\xofyp{7}{80}) and enterprise-level systems (\xofyp{2}{80}) receive comparatively little attention. Taken together, these findings indicate that dominant SoTS models and architectural choices are strongly shaped by the assumptions of industrial application domains. As a result, the transferability of existing SoTS designs to socio-technical settings—where system behavior is influenced by human decision-making, organizational structures, and institutional constraints—remains limited.

\begin{recoframe}{Recommendation}
Incorporate socio-technical considerations into SoTS design. In particular, researchers should examine alternative autonomy configurations, such as human-supervised and human-actuated DTs, to better understand how SoTS architectures operate in socio-technical contexts.
\end{recoframe}

To support cross-domain transferability, SoTS research should explore a wider range of application domains in order to identify design principles and architectural patterns that generalize beyond industrial settings. In particular, human-centered infrastructure and public-service domains provide opportunities to study SoTS under conditions where adaptation is shaped by organizational and social factors. Prior SoS research has long emphasized that SoS are inherently socio-technical systems, requiring explicit consideration of human and institutional roles alongside technical components \cite{siemieniuch2014extending}. Building on this perspective, we recommend that SoTS research adopt socio-technical DT methodologies that explicitly model human–technical interactions. Prior work advocates the use of agent-based modeling and simulation to capture human behavior, coordination, and adaptation alongside technical processes, and to treat humans and institutions as active system constituents rather than external users \cite{barn2022sociotechnical,ye2023developing}. In addition, socio-technical DT research emphasizes value-sensitive design principles, including participatory modeling, transparency, and accountability when representing human and organizational behavior \cite{barn2022sociotechnical}. Applied to SoTS research, these principles suggest prioritizing explicit modeling of human roles, organizational decision boundaries, and cross-domain interdependencies, as well as evaluating SoTS architectures based on their ability to support adaptation, coordination, and learning under evolving operating conditions \cite{ye2023developing}.
