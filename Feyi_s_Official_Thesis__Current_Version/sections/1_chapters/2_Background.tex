\section{Systems of Systems}\label{sec:background-sos}
A SoS is a collection of independent systems that collaborate to achieve outcomes unattainable by any single system. \citet{maier1998architecting} further define a SoS as a collection of operationally and managerially independent systems. Operational independence means each constituent system can function on its own, while managerial independence means they are developed and governed separately. These properties lead to systems being assembled from autonomous constituents, where participation is voluntary, and the overall behaviour emerges from their interactions \cite{boardman2006system}.

Several classifications describe how SoS differ based on how goals are defined and managed. In directed SoS, the system is created and centrally managed to achieve a specific goal. Constituent systems operate in support of that goal. In acknowledged SoS, there are shared system-level goals and a coordinating authority. However, constituent systems retain their own objectives and control, so changes must be negotiated. In collaborative SoS, systems voluntarily work together towards agreed goals. Decisions are made collectively rather than enforced. In virtual SoS, there is no central authority or agreed system-level goal. Any coordination arises informally across participating systems \cite{nielsen2015systems,dahmann2008systems}.

\subsection{Core Characteristics}
SoS exhibit specific properties due to their dynamic nature. \citet{nielsen2015systems} characterize these properties along eight dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Autonomy:} the extent to which constituent systems pursue their own objectives.
\item \textbf{Independence:} the ability of a constituent system to operate outside the SoS.
\item \textbf{Distribution:} the spatial and logical separation of constituent systems.
\item \textbf{Evolution:} changes in constituent systems and their relationships over time.
\item \textbf{Reconfiguration:} adjustments in the system composition during operation.
\item \textbf{Emergence:} behaviours arising from interactions among systems.
\item \textbf{Interdependence:} reliance between systems to achieve objectives.
\item \textbf{Interoperability:} the ability of heterogeneous systems to exchange information and services.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Constituent States in a SoS}\label{sec:background-sos-states}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/Background/SoS Lifecycle Axelsson.png}
\caption{Constituent State Model from \citet{axelsson2019refined}}
\label{fig:sos-constituent-state-model}
\end{figure}

\citet{axelsson2019refined} propose a state model that explains how systems participate in an SoS, shown in \figref{fig:sos-constituent-state-model}. A relevant system is any system with capabilities that may be useful to the SoS. If it does not meet the requirements, it remains in an \textit{ignorant} state and cannot participate. If it meets the required conditions, it becomes \textit{prepared}. This means it satisfies the technical or organizational requirements needed to join. Once it joins the SoS, it becomes a constituent system. A constituent system can be either \textit{passive} or \textit{active}. A passive system belongs to the SoS but is not currently contributing. An active system participates in a temporary collaboration, called a constellation. In this state, systems exchange information and work together to meet the SoS objectives.

\section{Digital Twins}\label{sec:background-dt}
A digital twin (DT) is a virtual representation of a physical system. A DT is continuously synchronized with its real-world counterpart through bidirectional data exchange~\cite{kritzinger2018digital}. This synchronization enables real-time decision-making and control of the physical asset. A DT system consists of three elements: the physical system, a virtual representation, and the connections between them~\cite{vanderhorn2021digital}. The virtual representation models the physical system using simulations, data-driven models, or a combination of both~\cite{negri2017review}. Data from the physical system is used to maintain an up-to-date model of its state. The DT also performs simulations, predictions, and analytics. It processes these to generate updates that can be applied back to the physical system through actuation~\cite{david2024infonomics}. This interaction forms a feedback loop, where information continuously flows between the physical and digital representations~\cite{tao2018digital}. \citet{kritzinger2018digital} further distinguish between three types of digital representations. Digital models have no data exchange with the physical system. Digital shadows support one-way data flow from physical system to the digital representation. DTs support bidirectional data exchange between the two.

Due to their ability to control complex physical systems in real-time, DTs have become key enablers of state-of-the-art cyber-physical systems, such as manufacturing systems~\cite{kritzinger2018digital}, autonomous vehicles~\cite{ramdhan2025engineering}, and critical infrastructure~\cite{liu2026introduction}, and smart ecosystems~\cite{michael2024smart}.

\subsection{Digital Twin Architectures}
DT architectures are often described using layered models. These separate the physical system, the digital representation, and the communication between them \cite{tao2018digital}. Some approaches extend this by adding layers for data management and services. These support functions such as analytics, monitoring, and lifecycle management \cite{tao2019digital}, sometimes more elaborate architectural aspects, such as complex constellations of physical and digital capabilities \cite{oakes2023digital}. In practice, industrial DT systems rely on infrastructure such as IoT platforms, cloud services, and simulation engines to support large-scale deployment \cite{redelinghuys2020six-layer}. Standardization efforts, such as RAMI~4.0 \cite{DIN91345} and ISO~23247 \cite{ISO23247-1}, provide reference architectures for organizing DT systems across lifecycle stages and functional layers of the DT architecture.

\subsection{Digital Twins in Distributed Systems}
DTs are increasingly used in distributed environments, where multiple physical systems interact and exchange information. In these settings, DTs support tasks such as monitoring, simulation, and predictive analysis across connected assets \cite{negri2017review, tao2018digital}.  Recent work looks at how multiple DTs can operate together. Federated DT models describe networks of independent twins that exchange information  \cite{vergara2023federated}. Hierarchical architectures organize multiple specialized twins that represent different parts of a larger system \cite{kunnari2025distributed}.  These approaches provide a foundation for coordinating multiple DTs within larger system structures.

\section{Reliability in Engineered Systems}\label{sec:background-reliability}
Reliability is a key attribute of dependability \cite{avizienis2004basic}. It is defined as the continuity of correct service \cite{avizienis2004basic}. It can also be expressed as the probability that a system performs its intended function without failure over a given period \cite{elsayed2021reliability}.  Software reliability engineering focuses on engineering reliable systems through modeling, testing, and fault handling techniques \cite{lyu2007software}. Traditionally, it has relied on failure data and probabilistic models to estimate system behaviour. However, these approaches are harder to apply in modern systems with complex interactions between software, hardware, and human components \cite{zio2009reliability}.

\subsection{Faults, Errors, and Failures}\label{sec:background-reliability-faults}
Dependability theory distinguishes between faults, errors, and failures \cite{avizienis2004basic}. A fault is the underlying cause of an abnormal condition. When activated, it produces an error, which is an incorrect internal state. If this error propagates to the system boundary and affects delivered service, a failure occurs. Faults can arise from hardware or software sources, including physical degradation, environmental conditions, or design and implementation defects. In complex systems, these faults may also originate from interactions between components \cite{ahmed2013survey}.

\begin{figure}[htp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.25\linewidth]{figures/Background/Parhami Impairement States.png}
\caption{A model of system reliability from \citet{parhami2002defect}}
\label{fig:impairment-states}
\end{figure}

\citet{parhami2002defect} describe system behaviour as progressing through levels of impairment, shown in \figref{fig:impairment-states}. A system is defective when it contains latent imperfections. When these are activated, the system becomes faulty, producing incorrect internal behaviour. If this affects system state or data, it becomes erroneous. When errors affect externally observable behaviour, the system is malfunctioning. Continued impact leads to a degraded state with reduced performance, and eventually a failed state where the system can no longer deliver its intended service.

\subsection{Approaches to Achieving Reliability}
Several approaches are used to achieve reliable system operation \cite{avizienis2004basic}. Fault prevention focuses on avoiding faults during design. Fault removal identifies and corrects faults through testing and verification. Fault forecasting estimates the likelihood and impact of failures. Fault tolerance allows systems to continue operating despite faults through mechanisms such as redundancy, monitoring, and recovery \cite{hanmer2013patterns}. Together, these approaches address different stages of faults, errors, and failures. Reliability depends on how effectively these mechanisms manage and mitigate impairments during system operation.

\section{Complex Event Processing}\label{sec:background-cep}
Complex event processing (CEP) analyzes streams of events to detect patterns and identify conditions in a system over time \cite{cugola2015complex}. It is commonly used in event-driven systems, where components continuously produce data that must be interpreted as it arrives. An event represents a discrete occurrence in a system or its environment \cite{etzion2010event}. CEP systems evaluate these streams against patterns that capture how events relate over time. This enables the detection of significant conditions by correlating multiple events and transforming low-level data into higher-level information that can be acted on \cite{cugola2012processing}. CEP systems typically use automata or state models to track partial matches and advance them as new events are processed. When the system fully matches a pattern, it emits an alert indicating that the condition has occurred \cite{cugola2015complex}. CEP was first introduced in early research systems and is now widely used in industrial engines and distributed stream processing frameworks \cite{cugola2012processing}. It is applied across domains such as logistics, finance, manufacturing, infrastructure monitoring, and healthcare. In these settings, it supports real-time detection of delays, anomalies, and failures, and triggers responses based on observed conditions \cite{dayarathna2018recent}.
