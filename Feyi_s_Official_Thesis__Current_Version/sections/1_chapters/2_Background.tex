\section{Digital Twins}\label{sec:background-digital-twin}
\subsection{Definitions}\label{subsec:dt-definitions}

A digital twin is a high-fidelity virtual representation of a physical asset or system. It maintains a continuous bi-directional connection with its physical counterpart to enable real-time synchronization, calibration, and actuation \citep{david2024infonomics}. Across definitions, the DT represents an advanced form of cyber-physical integration in which digital and physical entities co-evolve through data exchange and feedback loops \citep{tao2018digital, kritzinger2018digital}.

Definitions for DTs converge on a few core characteristics. First, a DT serves as a high-fidelity virtual counterpart to a physical system, designed to replicate its structure, state, and behaviour with a high degree of accuracy. Second, it relies on bi-directional communication between the physical and digital domains, ensuring that changes in one are reflected in the other through continuous synchronization and control feedback. Third, it depends on persistent data flow, achieved through IoT-enabled infrastructures, that keep the virtual model temporally aligned with its physical source. 


From a data exchange perspective, \citet{kritzinger2018digital} distinguish between three levels of representation in Digital Twin systems: the digital model, the digital shadow, and the digital twin. These levels describe the degree of integration between the physical and digital entities. A digital twin supports full bi-directional interaction, allowing it to both observe and actuate on its physical counterpart in real time. A digital shadow maintains a unidirectional connection in which information from the physical system is transmitted to the digital representation. This allows the digital model to reflect the current state of the asset but not to influence it. In contrast, a digital model is a static virtual representation that has no direct connection to the physical system.

\subsection{Historical Evolution}\label{subsec:dt-history}

The predecessor concept of using a “twin” for verification and testing originated in the aerospace domain. During NASA’s Apollo program in the 1960s, identical spacecraft were constructed so that engineers could mirror mission conditions on Earth and test alternative procedures in real time as flight data were received \citep{boschert2016digital}. This practice represented an early instance of using a physical counterpart to replicate operational conditions for simulation and fault diagnosis. A similar approach was later adopted in the aviation industry through “Iron Bird” test rigs, which physically replicated aircraft subsystems to validate system integration and control logic prior to flight \citep{boschert2016digital}. Together, these examples illustrate how the motivation to experiment with counterparts before acting on the real system has long been embedded in engineering practice, laying the conceptual foundation for modern data-driven digital twins.

The formal concept of the digital twin emerged in the early 2000s, building on earlier notions of digital mirroring. \citet{gelernter1993mirror} envisioned “mirror worlds” in which digital representations continuously reflected their physical counterparts, anticipating the synchronization principles later realized in digital twins. In the early 2000s, \citet{grieves2005product} introduced the digital twin within the context of product lifecycle management (PLM), emphasizing the bidirectional coupling between physical systems and virtual models to enable continuous alignment throughout design, manufacturing, and operation. Around the same time, \citet{hribernik2006product} proposed the concept of a “product avatar,” linking digital representations to lifecycle integration through metadata and interoperability. NASA further refined the concept for aerospace applications, defining a digital twin as a high-fidelity, probabilistic simulation system capable of predicting system behaviour, performance degradation, and maintenance requirements across an asset’s lifecycle \citep{nasaprocessroadmap, glaessgen2012digital}.

With the rise of Industry~4.0, digital twins emerged as a key enabler of cyber-physical integration. By linking physical systems, sensors, and machines through cloud and IoT infrastructures, digital twins enable real-time analytics and optimization \citep{liu2021review}. Industrial platforms such as GE’s \textit{Predix}~\citep{gepredixoverview} and Siemens’ \textit{MindSphere}~\citep{siemensmindsphere} exemplified this transition from theory to large-scale implementation, creating continuously updated digital counterparts for industrial assets. In academia, research paralleled these developments by exploring digital twin reference architectures, interoperability standards, and domain-specific implementations across healthcare, transportation, and smart cities \citep{barricelli2019survey, botin2022digital}.

Overall, the evolution of the digital twin reflects a progression from simulation-based design tools toward data-driven systems that integrate sensing, computation, and actuation. Each stage of this progression—from early aerospace simulations to Industry~4.0—has increased the degree of automation, fidelity, and intelligence achievable within digital representations.


\subsection{Architectures}\label{subsec:dt-architectures}

Digital twin architectures describe how the physical and digital domains are coupled through data, models, and services. They provide a conceptual framework for defining the components, data flows, and feedback mechanisms. As DT implementations expanded across domains, several reference models and standards emerged to formalize their structure, functionality, and interoperability.

\citet{tao2018digital} proposed one of the most influential architectural models, defining the digital twin as a five-dimensional construct composed of the physical entity, virtual entity, connection, data, and services. This framework emphasizes the iterative feedback loops between the physical and virtual entities, where data flow continuously through the connection and data dimensions to support real-time synchronization, simulation, and optimization. This model highlights the DT as a closed information loop in which the physical and virtual components co-evolve through data-driven feedback.

International bodies have formalized DT architectures through initiatives such as ISO~23247 \citep{ISO23247-1} and RAMI~4.0 \citep{DIN91345}. ISO~23247 defines a reference framework for DTs within manufacturing, specifying roles, entities, and data flows across four hierarchical layers. RAMI~4.0 similarly organizes industrial systems along three axes: hierarchical levels, lifecycle stages, and interoperability layers. This multidimensional structure connects DT functionality with broader Industry~4.0 principles. Together, these standards establish a shared vocabulary and design foundation for scalable, interoperable DT implementations across sectors.
