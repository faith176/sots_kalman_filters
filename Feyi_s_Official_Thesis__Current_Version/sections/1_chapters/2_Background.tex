\section{Digital Twins}\label{sec:background-dt}
Digital twins are central to the digital transformation of cyber-physical systems. They serve as dynamic virtual counterparts to physical assets, providing continuous synchronization and enabling monitoring, simulation, and control. 
The following subsections outline their history, distinguishing characteristics, and applications.

\subsection{Definitions and Core Characteristics}\label{subsec:background-dt-definitions}
% Define the DT concept across major sources (Grieves 2002; Tao et al. 2018; Kritzinger et al. 2018).
% Highlight core properties: physical–digital pairing, synchronization, fidelity, and feedback.
% Mention how definitions converge on the idea of real-time, bi-directional linkage.

A digital twin is broadly defined as a high-fidelity virtual representation of a physical asset or system that remains continuously synchronized with its real-world counterpart through bi-directional data exchange and feedback control \cite{david2024infonomics}. 
It extends the concept of cyber-physical integration by enabling physical and digital entities to co-evolve: data from the physical system updates the virtual model, while the model can influence the physical system through predictive or corrective actions \cite{tao2018digital}. 
This synchronization, supported by IoT-enabled sensing and networked infrastructures, allows the DT to replicate not only the structure and state of its physical twin but also its dynamic behaviour in real time.

Across definitions, the literature converges on three primary components that together constitute a Digital Twin: 
(1) a \textbf{physical reality}, representing the system, environment, and processes of interest; 
(2) a \textbf{virtual representation}, capturing an abstraction of that reality through computational and data models; and 
(3) \textbf{interconnections} that enable continuous information exchange between the two domains \cite{vanderhorn2021digital}. 
The physical reality encompasses the system and its operating context and environment. The virtual representation mirrors this reality at a chosen level of abstraction, using models that combine physics-based and data-driven methods to simulate system behaviour and state evolution. 
The interconnections between them supports two complementary flows: the \textit{physical-to-virtual} direction, which updates the virtual model with sensor or observational data, and the \textit{virtual-to-physical} direction, which projects insights or control actions back to the real system \cite{vanderhorn2021digital}. 
Together, these flows establish the continuous synchronization that distinguishes Digital Twins from conventional simulation or monitoring models.

\citet{kritzinger2018digital} further clarify this distinction by defining three levels of digital representation: the \textit{digital model}, the \textit{digital shadow}, and the \textit{digital twin}. 
A digital model is a static representation without live data connectivity. 
A digital shadow supports one-way information flow, updating the digital entity from the physical system but not vice versa. 
A digital twin, in contrast, achieves full bi-directional interaction, enabling real-time monitoring, simulation, and control. 
This bidirectional feedback loop is what transforms the DT from a descriptive model into an operational entity capable of influencing physical outcomes.




\subsection{History of DTs}\label{subsec:background-dt-history}
The predecessor concept of using a “twin” for verification and testing originated in the aerospace domain. During NASA’s Apollo program in the 1960s, an identical spacecraft was constructed so that engineers could mirror mission conditions on Earth and test alternative procedures in real time as flight data were received \cite{boschert2016digital}. This practice represented an early instance of using a physical counterpart to replicate operational conditions for simulation and fault diagnosis. A similar approach was later adopted in the aviation industry through “Iron Bird” test rigs, which physically replicated aircraft subsystems to validate system integration and control logic prior to flight \cite{boschert2016digital}. Together, these examples illustrate how the motivation to experiment with counterparts before acting on the real system has long been embedded in engineering practice, laying the conceptual foundation for modern data-driven digital twins.

The formal concept of the digital twin emerged in the early 2000s, building on earlier notions of digital mirroring. \citet{gelernter1993mirror} envisioned “mirror worlds” in which digital representations continuously reflected their physical counterparts, anticipating the synchronization principles later realized in digital twins. In the early 2000s, \citet{grieves2005product} introduced the digital twin within the context of product lifecycle management (PLM), emphasizing the bidirectional coupling between physical systems and virtual models to enable continuous alignment throughout design, manufacturing, and operation. Around the same time, \citet{hribernik2006product} proposed the concept of a “product avatar,” linking digital representations to lifecycle integration through metadata and interoperability. NASA further refined the concept for aerospace applications, defining a digital twin as a high-fidelity, probabilistic simulation system capable of predicting system behaviour, performance degradation, and maintenance requirements across an asset’s lifecycle \cite{nasaprocessroadmap, glaessgen2012digital}.

With the rise of Industry~4.0, digital twins emerged as a key enabler of cyber-physical integration. By linking physical systems, sensors, and machines through cloud and IoT infrastructures, digital twins enable real-time analytics and optimization \cite{liu2021review}. Industrial platforms such as GE’s Predix~\cite{gepredixoverview} and Siemens’ MindSphere~\cite{siemensmindsphere} exemplified this transition from theory to large-scale implementation, creating continuously updated digital counterparts for industrial assets. In academia, research paralleled these developments by exploring digital twin reference architectures, interoperability standards, and domain-specific implementations across healthcare, transportation, and smart cities \cite{barricelli2019survey, botin2022digital}.

Overall, the evolution of the digital twin reflects a progression from simulation-based design tools toward data-driven systems that integrate sensing, computation, and actuation. Each stage of this progression, from early aerospace simulations to Industry~4.0, has increased the degree of automation, fidelity, and intelligence achievable within digital representations.

DT architectures formalize how physical systems, data infrastructures, and computational models interact to enable synchronization and control across the system lifecycle. Over the past decade, several layered and reference architectures have emerged, each extending the conceptual scope and technological maturity of DT design.

Early conceptualizations of DTs adopted a three-layer structure consisting of the physical, virtual, and connection layers \cite{grieves2014digital, tao2018digital}. The physical layer represents real-world assets equipped with sensors, actuators, and controllers that generate operational data. The virtual layer maintains a digital representation that mirrors system behavior to support analysis and control, while the connection layer enables bi-directional communication and data exchange between the two. Although this model provided a foundational view of cyber-physical integration, it offered limited guidance on data management, services, and lifecycle evolution.

To address these gaps, later frameworks expanded the architecture to incorporate additional layers. \citet{tao2019digital} proposed a five-layer model that introduced dedicated data and service components. In this model, the physical entity forms the basis for constructing the virtual entity that supports simulation and decision-making. The data layer functions as the core of the system, enabling collection, fusion, and knowledge generation, while the service layer provides optimization, maintenance, and user interaction capabilities. The connection layer bridges all components to maintain synchronization and feedback.

\citet{redelinghuys2020six} proposed a six-layer architecture that further integrates data exchange, cloud connectivity, and simulation intelligence through open, vendor-neutral interfaces. The lower layers encompass physical devices and local data repositories, the middle layers introduce an IoT gateway that converts sensor data into structured information for cloud storage and analytics, and the upper layers host emulation and simulation tools for real-time visualization and control. By distributing data acquisition, transformation, and reasoning across interconnected layers, the architecture enhances scalability and interoperability within complex industrial environments.

Parallel to academic developments, standardization initiatives have formalized DT concepts into reference frameworks. The Reference Architectural Model for Industry 4.0 (RAMI 4.0) provides a structured model for organizing DT-enhanced industrial systems across three dimensions: hierarchy levels, product lifecycle stages, and architecture layers \cite{DIN91345}. Together, these dimensions describe how physical assets, digital systems, and business processes interact throughout the system lifecycle. Within this structure, RAMI 4.0 serves as the reference model defining the structural and functional layers of Industry 4.0 systems, while the DT provides the implementation mechanism that realizes these principles in practice. DTs align the hierarchy, lifecycle, and functional dimensions of RAMI 4.0, enabling continuous synchronization between operational processes and enterprise decision-making.

Building on these principles, the international standard ISO 23247 \cite{ISO23247-1} defines a Digital Twin reference architecture for manufacturing structured around four interconnected domains: the observable manufacturing domain, the device communication domain, the digital twin domain, and the user domain. The observable manufacturing domain contains the physical assets and processes being monitored, while the device communication domain ensures secure and reliable data transfer between physical and digital components. The digital twin domain manages models, analytics, and synchronization, and the user domain provides visualization and decision support interfaces. The architecture is governed by a Digital Twin Framework that specifies functional entities and standardized interfaces for data acquisition, communication, and synchronization. By defining explicit roles and message exchanges between these entities, ISO 23247 enables modular and interoperable DT implementations across heterogeneous industrial environments.

DT architectures have since been applied across a wide range of domains—including manufacturing, energy, healthcare, transportation, and smart cities—to enhance operational efficiency and system resilience \cite{hu2021digital}. \citet{shao2021use} describe several representative implementations developed within the NIST Smart Manufacturing Systems Test Bed. A machine health DT uses process and equipment data to monitor, diagnose, and predict faults in manufacturing assets, enabling proactive maintenance through data-driven recommendations or automated control adjustments. A scheduling and routing DT integrates information from shop-floor systems such as production equipment, MES, and ERP platforms to optimize routing flexibility, cycle times, and resource utilization under varying demand conditions. Finally, a virtual commissioning DT employs simulation and control logic emulation to design and validate production systems prior to physical deployment, minimizing commissioning costs and reducing post-installation risks. Collectively, these applications demonstrate the adaptability of the DT paradigm, showing how real-time synchronization and continuous feedback enhance reliability, efficiency, and decision-making across diverse socio-technical contexts.
