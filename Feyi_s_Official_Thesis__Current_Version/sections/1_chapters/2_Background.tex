\chapter{Background}\label{ch:background}
This chapter introduces the foundational concepts required for the remainder of the thesis, including systems of systems, digital twins, reliability in engineered systems, and complex event processing.

\section{Systems of Systems}
A system of systems (SoS) is a collection of independent systems that collaborate to achieve outcomes unattainable by any single constituent \cite{maier1998architecting}.

\subsection{Definitions and Types}\label{subsec:background-sos-definitions}

Engineered systems are commonly understood as collections of interacting components whose behaviour emerges from their interactions rather than from the properties of individual parts \cite{von1950theory}. As system scale increases, these interactions extend across independently developed and operated systems, motivating the study of systems composed of cooperating subsystems. \citet{maier1998architecting} defined a system of systems as a collection of operationally and managerially independent systems that collaborate to achieve outcomes beyond their individual capabilities. Operational independence refers to the ability of constituent systems to operate outside the SoS, while managerial independence reflects that constituent systems are developed and governed by different authorities. These properties lead to decentralized control, evolving participation, and coordination among heterogeneous systems.

Several classifications describe how SoS are organized according to the degree of centralized coordination. Directed SoS are centrally managed to achieve a specific purpose while constituent systems retain independent operation. Acknowledged SoS have recognized objectives and coordination mechanisms but maintain independent ownership and development of constituent systems. In collaborative SoS, systems cooperate to achieve shared goals without centralized authority. Virtual SoS lack centralized management and explicit system-level objectives \cite{nielsen2015systems,dahmann2008systems}.

\subsection{Core Characteristics}

Systems of systems exhibit structural and behavioural properties associated with the interaction of independent systems. \citet{boardman2006system} describe SoS as purposeful organizations of systems connected through decentralized authority and shared objectives. \citet{nielsen2015systems} synthesized several prior perspectives---including \cite{boulding1956general,ackoff1971towards,pei2000system,boardman2006system,keating2003system,kotov1999systems,maier1998architecting,shenhar19952}---into an eight-dimensional taxonomy for analyzing SoS.

The taxonomy identifies the following dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Autonomy:} the extent to which constituent systems pursue their own objectives.
\item \textbf{Independence:} the ability of a constituent system to operate outside the SoS.
\item \textbf{Distribution:} the spatial and logical separation of constituent systems.
\item \textbf{Evolution:} changes in constituent systems and their relationships over time.
\item \textbf{Reconfiguration:} adjustments in system relationships during operation.
\item \textbf{Emergence:} behaviours arising from interactions among systems.
\item \textbf{Interdependence:} reliance between systems to achieve system-level objectives.
\item \textbf{Interoperability:} the ability of heterogeneous systems to exchange information and services.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Lifecycle of Constituents in a System of Systems}
To describe how systems participate in an SoS, \citet{axelsson2019refined} proposed a model describing the states that a system may occupy relative to the SoS.

\begin{figure}[htp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/Background/SoS Lifecycle Axelsson.png}
\caption{Constituent State Model from \cite{axelsson2019refined}}
\label{fig:sos-constituent-state-model}
\end{figure}

In this model, a relevant system is any system possessing capabilities that may contribute to the SoS. When the system satisfies the technical or organizational requirements for participation, it becomes \textit{prepared}. When a prepared system joins the SoS, it becomes a constituent system. Constituent systems may occupy either passive or active states depending on their current participation. A \textit{passive} system belongs to the SoS but is not currently engaged in delivering a system-level capability, whereas an \textit{active} system participates in a temporary collaboration structure, referred to as a constellation, through which SoS capabilities are produced.

\section{Digital Twins}\label{sec:background-dt}

A digital twin (DT) is a dynamic virtual counterpart of a physical asset that remains continuously synchronized through real-time data exchange \cite{kritzinger2018digital}.

\subsection{Definitions and Core Characteristics}
A DT is a virtual representation of a physical asset that remains synchronized with its real-world counterpart through bidirectional data exchange \cite{david2024infonomics}. Data from the physical system updates the digital representation, while the digital model generates simulations, predictions, or optimization outputs that can be fed back to support monitoring, control, and decision-making. This interaction forms a closed-loop process in which information continuously flows between the physical system and its digital counterpart \cite{tao2018digital}.

\citet{vanderhorn2021digital} describe a DT as consisting of three elements: a physical entity, a virtual representation, and the interconnections that link them. The physical entity corresponds to the real system and its operating environment, while the virtual representation models aspects of that system using computational approaches that may combine physics-based simulations, data-driven models, or hybrid methods \cite{negri2017review}. The interconnections, supported by sensing technologies and networked communication infrastructures, enable bidirectional information exchange, including physical-to-virtual data transfer that updates the digital representation and virtual-to-physical communication that provides feedback or control information.

\citet{kritzinger2018digital} further classify digital representations into three categories: digital models, which have no automated data exchange with the physical system; digital shadows, which support one-way data flow from physical to digital; and DTs, which support bidirectional data exchange between the two.

\subsection{Digital Twin Architectures}
DT architectures are commonly described using layered models that separate physical systems, digital representations, and communication infrastructure \cite{tao2018digital}. Extensions introduce additional layers for data management and services, supporting analytics, monitoring, and lifecycle management \cite{tao2019digital}. Industrial implementations incorporate infrastructure such as IoT platforms, cloud services, and simulation engines to support large-scale deployments \cite{redelinghuys2020six}. Standardization efforts, including RAMI~4.0 \cite{DIN91345} and ISO~23247 \cite{ISO23247-1}, define reference architectures for structuring DT systems across lifecycle stages and functional layers.

\subsection{Digital Twins in Distributed Systems}

DTs are increasingly deployed in distributed environments where multiple physical systems interact and exchange information. In such settings, DTs support monitoring, simulation, and predictive analysis across interconnected assets \cite{negri2017review, tao2018digital}. Recent research explores architectures that integrate multiple DTs operating within shared environments. Federated DT models describe networks of autonomous twins that exchange information while maintaining independence \cite{vergara2023federated}, while hierarchical architectures organize multiple specialized twins representing subsystems of a larger infrastructure \cite{kunnari2025distributed}. These developments provide the foundation for coordinating multiple DTs within larger system structures.

\section{Reliability in Engineered Systems}\label{sec:background-reliability}

Reliability is a fundamental attribute of dependability that characterises a system's ability to sustain correct operation over time \cite{avizienis2004basic}.

\subsection{Definition of Reliability}
Dependability theory describes the trustworthiness of engineered systems as the justified confidence that a system delivers correct service under stated conditions \cite{avizienis2004basic}. It encompasses attributes such as availability, reliability, safety, integrity, and maintainability. Within this framework, reliability refers to the continuity of correct service, which is the ability of a system to operate without failure over a specified time interval. In reliability engineering, this is commonly expressed as the probability that a system performs its intended function without failure for a given period \cite{ram2013system, alma991032896202207371}. System operation can be viewed as alternating between correct and incorrect service, where failures interrupt normal behaviour \cite{laprie1996software}. Accordingly, reliability is characterised through the failure process, capturing the occurrence and timing of failures over time. It is inherently time-dependent and is described using concepts such as time to failure and failure rate, with summary measures including mean time to failure (MTTF) and mean time between failures (MTBF).

\subsection{Faults, Errors, and Failures}
Dependability theory distinguishes between faults, errors, and failures to describe how impairments propagate within a system \cite{avizienis2004basic}. A fault is the underlying cause of an abnormal condition; when activated, it produces an error, corresponding to an incorrect internal state. If this error propagates to the system boundary and affects delivered service, a failure occurs. Faults may originate from hardware or software sources, including physical degradation, environmental influences, or design and implementation defects, and may arise across interacting components in complex systems \cite{ahmed2013survey}.

\begin{figure}[htp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.25\linewidth]{figures/Background/Parhami Impairement States.png}
\caption{Multi-level model of reliability from \cite{parhami2002defect}}
\label{fig:impairment-states}
\end{figure}

To characterise the impact of such impairments, \citet{parhami2002defect} describe system behaviour in terms of progressive levels of impairment, where a system transitions through states reflecting increasing degradation of operation. A system is defective when it contains latent imperfections. When activated, these lead to a faulty state with incorrect internal behaviour. If faults contaminate system state or data, the system becomes erroneous. When errors affect externally observable behaviour, the system is malfunctioning. Continued impact results in a degraded state with reduced performance, and ultimately a failed state where intended service can no longer be delivered.


\subsection{Approaches to Achieving Reliability}
Several engineering approaches are described in the literature for achieving dependable system operation \cite{avizienis2004basic}. Fault prevention aims to avoid introducing faults during system design through disciplined engineering methods. Fault removal identifies and corrects faults through testing, verification, and maintenance activities. Fault forecasting estimates the likelihood and consequences of failures using reliability models and risk analysis. Fault tolerance enables systems to continue operating despite faults through mechanisms such as redundancy, monitoring, and recovery, allowing systems to detect, isolate, and mitigate the impact of faults during operation \cite{hanmer2013patterns}. These approaches collectively address different stages in the lifecycle of faults, errors, and failures. In this context, reliability depends on how effectively such mechanisms manage and mitigate these impairments during system operation.


\section{Complex Event Processing}\label{sec:background-cep}
Event-driven processing is a computational model in which system behaviour advances in response to events \cite{kommera2020power}. An event represents a discrete occurrence within a system or its environment, typically modeled as a record with a payload and timestamp \cite{etzion2010event}. Events are produced asynchronously by sources such as sensors or software components and are collected into event streams that capture system behaviour over time. CEP extends this model by enabling the detection of higher-level situations through the interpretation and correlation of events over time \cite{cugola2015complex}. CEP systems evaluate continuous event streams against declarative patterns specifying temporal and logical relationships, such as sequencing, aggregation, and time windows \cite{cugola2012processing,cugola2015complex}. When a pattern is satisfied, a composite event is emitted representing a meaningful system-level condition. To support incremental pattern detection, CEP engines maintain internal state to track partial matches over time, commonly using automata-based or state-machine-based execution models. These approaches introduce trade-offs between expressiveness, memory usage, and performance \cite{cugola2015complex}.

CEP originated in academic systems such as SASE and Cayuga \cite{wu2006high,brenna2007cayuga}, and was later adopted in industrial engines such as Esper and Siddhi and integrated into distributed stream processing frameworks such as Apache Flink \cite{esper_reference,suhothayan2011siddhi,carbone2015apache,dayarathna2018recent}. CEP has been applied across domains including logistics, finance, manufacturing, infrastructure monitoring, and healthcare \cite{buchmann2009complex,manchana2021event}. CEP is used to detect and interpret patterns in event streams generated by distributed and event-driven systems \cite{kommera2020power,dayarathna2018recent}.
