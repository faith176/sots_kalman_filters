\section{Key Takeaway for the Future of SoTS}\label{sec:literature-review-key-takeaways}
This section synthesizes the literature review findings to identify key gaps limiting current SoTS research and practice.
%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Reliability is predominantly addressed through service-level mechanisms}

Reliability is one of the most frequently addressed non-functional properties in SoTS, appearing in over half of the sampled studies (\xofyp{41}{80}, see \tabref{tab:reliability-table}). However, it is primarily handled through service-level mechanisms such as falling back to locally hosted or lightweight DT instances during communication loss~\cite{alam2017c2ps,larsen2024towards}, using asynchronous communication to tolerate intermittent updates~\cite{acharya2023twins,liu2020web-based}, and employing runtime fault recovery strategies~\cite{esterle2021digital,villalonga2021decision-making}. These approaches indicate that reliability is treated as a runtime concern, focusing on keeping the system operational rather than being explicitly defined as a formal system property.

Despite its prevalence, reliability is rarely modeled explicitly (\xofyp{2}{80}) and is only occasionally evaluated (\xofyp{3}{80}), typically through simulation or fault injection~\cite{park2020digital,saraeian2022digital}. This suggests that current approaches lack formal representations of reliability for SoTS, limiting the ability to reason about system behavior or compare reliability across systems. As a result, reliability considerations remain system-specific rather than systematically defined.

\begin{recoframe}{Recommendation 1}
Develop formal models and evaluation approaches for reliability in SoTS to enable systematic specification, analysis, and comparison of reliability across systems.
\end{recoframe}

\subsection{On the need for SoTS architectures}
\input{tables/Literature Review/RQs/rq1/challenges_table.tex}
One of the key challenges in DT engineering is the lack of established architectures~\cite{ferko2022architecting}, a limitation that carries over to SoTS. As shown in \tabref{tab:challenges-table}, both the lack of architectures and standards emerge as recurring design challenges. Given that the primary intent of SoTS is the organization of DTs into SoS (\tabref{tab:intents-table}), there is a clear need for architectures that can accommodate SoS dynamics, particularly interoperability, which is highlighted in nearly 40\% of studies (\tabref{tab:challenges-table}).

The dominance of acknowledged and directed SoS types (over 70\% in \tabref{tab:sots-type-table}) suggests that current SoTS struggle to support more dynamic configurations, with collaborative and virtual SoS appearing in less than 30\% of cases. This limitation is likely tied to the absence of architectural specifications and standards. While structural modeling approaches such as SysML and UML class diagrams are frequently used (\tabref{tab:modeling-methods-structured-table}), these efforts remain insufficiently consolidated into architectural frameworks.

Developing SoTS architectures should therefore be a priority, as it would improve both system and research maturity (see \tabref{tab:evaluation-structured-table} and \tabref{tab:trl-table}). Promising directions include microservice architectures~\cite{bellavista2023requirements}, co-simulation via FMI/FMU~\cite{bottjer2023review}, and DT interoperability~\cite{david2024interoperability}. Our classification framework (\secref{sec:literature-review-classification}) provides a foundation for these efforts.

\begin{recoframe}{Recommendation 2}
Develop architectural specifications and reference implementations for SoTS to ease their engineering and to allow higher levels of maturity in their research and development.
\end{recoframe}


\subsection{The lack of standards is a key challenge in SoTS}

Standardization remains underdeveloped in SoTS. Fewer than half of the studies rely on standards (\xofyp{36}{80}{}, \tabref{tab:standards-table}), and those that do primarily adopt data exchange standards such as OPC UA, the Asset Administration Shell (IEC 63278), and RAMI 4.0~\cite{shao2024manufacturing}. These are not specifically designed for SoTS, and standards are explicitly cited as a challenge (\tabref{tab:challenges-table}), reinforcing the need identified in architectural research~\cite{ferko2022architecting}.

This gap is particularly critical in domains such as automotive systems and smart cities (\tabref{tab:domains-table}), where standardization is essential for adoption. Current DT standards, such as ISO 23247, are limited in supporting dynamic systems~\cite{liu2024ai}, while SoS standardization remains immature. However, upcoming extensions to ISO 23247 (Parts 5 and 6) targeting digital threads and DT composition may address key SoTS challenges, including interoperability and synchronization~\cite{shao2024manufacturing}.

\begin{recoframe}{Recommendation 3}
Develop standards for DT and SoS, and participate in standardization efforts to improve the maturity of SoTS.
\end{recoframe}


\subsection{Managing emergent behavior in SoS by DTs}

Emergent behavior is a defining characteristic of SoS, yet current SoTS approaches remain largely limited to acknowledged and directed configurations (\tabref{tab:sots-type-table}). We hypothesize that DTs can enable more advanced management of emergence due to their tight cyber-physical coupling. This aligns with the concept of active experimentation~\cite{zeigler2018theory}, recently revitalized through DTs~\cite{mittal2023towards,barat2022digital}, where system behavior is intentionally perturbed to reveal insights. Such approaches have been explored in domains including uncrewed aerial systems~\cite{guo2023intelligent}, autonomous driving~\cite{pun2023neural}, and AI simulation~\cite{liu2024ai}.

Even after purposeful experimentation, some uncertainty about the behavior of the SoTS remains. To manage these unknown unknowns~\cite{ramasesh2014conceptual}, we recommend researching computing techniques that have the potential to react to unknown unknowns better, e.g., faster-than-real-time simulations to react to emergence faster or to anticipate it on a short time horizon; and using sound modeling techniques, such as goal modeling (e.g., via I*~\cite{goncalves2018systematic} and KAOS~\cite{goncalves2025systematic}) to codify the expected behavior of SoTS.


\begin{recoframe}{Recommendation 4}
Leverage DT capabilities to understand and manage emergent behavior in SoS, e.g., by purposeful experimentation with physical systems, or by improving run-time modeling\&simulation capabilities.
\end{recoframe}


\subsection{Empirical evaluations are lagging behind}

While technical contributions dominate the literature (\tabref{tab:contribution-type-table}), many studies remain at moderate maturity levels. Over half exceed demo prototype TRL (\tabref{tab:trl-table}), with technical contributions clustering around TRLs 5--6 and occasional deployments at TRLs 7--8. Case studies are fewer but tend to reach higher maturity, including one at TRL 9 (\figref{fig:trl-v-cont}). This indicates strong opportunities for empirical research. We encourage reporting through case reports and exemplars~\cite{mezard2007where}, particularly in industry-focused venues. Recommended methodologies include case studies~\cite{wohlin2021case}, design science~\cite{dresch2015design}, action research~\cite{cohen2017research}, and ethnography~\cite{sharp2010using} for human-centered aspects. Such work will improve research maturity by shifting from validation-heavy approaches (currently 90\%) toward more rigorous evaluation methods (\tabref{tab:evaluation-structured-table}), in line with \textcite{petersen2015guidelines}.

\begin{recoframe}{Recommendation 5}
Conduct empirical inquiries into SoTS by using established methods, such as case studies and action research.
\end{recoframe}
