\section{Implications for SoTS Engineering Practice}\label{sec:discussion-implications}
This section outlines how lifecycle-based modelling affects the engineering and management of SoTS.

\subsection{Lifecycle Modelling Improves SoTS Engineering Maturity}

State-based lifecycle modelling can be used throughout SoTS engineering, from early design to runtime operation. Frameworks such as Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI) describe how processes evolve from informal practices to defined and controlled approaches \cite{paulk1991capability}. At early stages, lifecycle models provide a shared representation of participation in SoTS, improving consistency across teams. As maturity increases, they can be treated as engineering artefacts, similar to requirements or architectural models, supporting versioning, review, and validation. This enables formal specification and validation of behaviour, followed by automated code generation from validated models.

Adopting this approach requires modelling capabilities and system-level design expertise to manage evolving lifecycle definitions. It may introduce roles such as SoTS architects or model engineers responsible for maintaining these models. As shared reference points, executable lifecycle models reduce ambiguity, improve communication, and support more controlled engineering processes.


\subsection{Lifecycle Models Enforce System-Level Contracts in SoTS}
Enforcing lifecycle constraints through the proposed model can be viewed as enforcing contracts between DT constituents and the SoTS. States capture the conditions for valid participation and contributions, while transitions define permitted behavioural changes. This aligns with contract-based design \cite{benveniste2012contracts}, where behaviour is defined through assumptions and guarantees, and is realized here through state-based models that both describe and enforce valid interaction across independently evolving constituents. Work on contract specifications shows that they can be transformed into state-based models \cite{bae2016systematic}, supporting runtime enforcement through states and transitions.

This has direct implications for how SoTS are engineered. Lifecycle models can be treated as executable contract specifications that can be shared across stakeholders, reviewed, and versioned as part of the engineering process, providing a consistent reference for expected behaviour. Because they are executable, they enable automatic enforcement at runtime, reducing the need for manual coordination logic within individual systems \cite{sangiovanni2012taming}. This simplifies integration, as new constituents only need to satisfy the lifecycle contract to participate, and reduces ambiguity during development and evolution by making assumptions and guarantees explicit and enforceable \cite{waschle2021contract}.



\subsection{Lifecycle Models Decouple SoTS Coordination from Service Logi}
The proposed approach separates constituent internals from system-level coordination by externalising SoTS concerns into lifecycle models. DT constituents participate through well-defined states and transitions without exposing internal behaviour or implementation details. Behaviour is controlled by a shared model rather than embedded within individual components. This keeps coordination at the SoTS level and allows constituents to focus on local functionality. As a result, constituents can evolve independently---being modified, replaced, or reconfigured---without global redesign. This aligns with operational and managerial independence in SoS \cite{maier1998architecting} and architectural principles that limit exposure to stakeholder-relevant concerns \cite{iso42010}. It supports scalable integration of heterogeneous DTs while maintaining system-level consistency.

This separation also reduces information exposure and integration complexity. Interaction is mediated through lifecycle states, ensuring that internal models do not need to account for external system dynamics. This aligns with privacy perspectives such as the NIST Privacy Framework \cite{enterprise2020nist}, where risks are addressed through system design across the data lifecycle. By constraining participation based on the state of constituents, the model limits unnecessary data flows and unintended exposure. This is especially important in SoS settings, where interactions between independent systems introduce additional security, privacy, and trust risks \cite{olivero2024systematic}. It also clarifies engineering responsibilities: SoTS-level developers focus on coordination and guarantees, while service-level developers focus on local behaviour. This reduces cognitive load and supports more manageable SoTS development.


\section{Future Directions}\label{sec:discussion-future}
This section identifies opportunities to extend the model to support additional concerns and more complex system behaviour.


\subsection{Representing Additional Concerns as Orthogonal Regions}
\secref{sec:two-dimensional-disturbances} introduced a two-dimensional model of SoTS disturbances based on belonging and health. While these dimensions support reasoning about reliability, they do not capture all factors that influence participation in the SoTS. In practice, additional concerns such as trust, authorization level, confidentiality, and data integrity also affect whether and how DTs interact \cite{li2023trust, kuruppuarachchi2023trusted}. These concerns are well recognized in distributed DT settings, where challenges related to data sharing, access control, and inconsistent interpretation of information directly affect which DTs are suitable for collaboration \cite{tripathi2024stakeholders}. These concerns can be incorporated into the SoTS model as orthogonal regions, where each region captures a distinct aspect influencing participation. This allows participation to be determined based on multiple system-level factors rather than belonging and health alone.

However, expanding the model introduces additional complexity. In settings involving multiple organizations, stakeholders must agree on how these concerns are defined, what their states represent, and how they influence eligibility to contribute \cite{carney2005topics}. Incorporating them allows for more precise control over contributions, but requires clear reasoning about how the regions interact. This extends beyond reliability and highlights the need to account for additional dependability attributes \cite{avizienis2004basic}.


\subsection{Dynamic Fault Tolerance in SoTS}
At the highest reliability level (\secref{sec:lv4}), the model allows constituents to continue participating in the SoTS under a restricted role when their condition degrades. This is realized in \secref{sec:demo-compensation} through a compensation component that estimates missing data. Different fault tolerance strategies can be applied in these situations \cite{hanmer2013patterns}. Each introduces trade-offs, particularly in terms of resource usage and predictability \cite{narasimhan2002trade}. In dynamic systems, fault tolerance must adapt to changing conditions, and selecting strategies at runtime allows the system to respond more effectively than fixed approaches \cite{de2022systematic, ballesteros2022infrastructure}.

The proposed SoTS model supports runtime strategy selection based on the current system state by adding an additional orthogonal region to the statechart, where each state represents a strategy and substates capture variations. Transitions and guards are influenced by changes in other regions, enabling adaptive behaviour. For example, state estimation can handle intermittent missing data, while more severe degradation can trigger a switch to data fusion using observations from nearby DTs. Because statecharts are executable, learning-based methods such as reinforcement learning can update transitions and guards based on observed outcomes \cite{zhang2020fault, dong2023deep}. This extends the model from enforcing allowable configurations to selecting appropriate response strategies.


\subsection{Formal Verification of SoTS}
Through adopting a state based modelling approach to controlling SoTS (\secref{sec:statecharts-reliability}), we gain the ability to formally verify system behaviour. Models allow system behaviour to be represented explicitly, enabling formal verification through systematic analysis of all possible system executions. It becomes possible to reason about how the system can evolve under all different conditions. This allows properties such as safety (forbidden configurations are never reached), liveness (desired progression occurs), and invariants over belonging and health to be checked with guarantees \cite{henzinger2023quantitative}. This can be supported through the integration of formal analysis tools into the modelling workflow, enabling automated verification as the system evolves \cite{drusinsky2011modeling}.

Further analysis can be supported through model-driven engineering (MDE) techniques. Behaviour is defined once in the statechart and systematically transformed into other models for analysis \cite{biehl2010literature}. These transformations provide alternative views of the same behaviour, making properties visible that are not explicit in the original model \cite{bezivin2006model}. For example, while statecharts capture the lifecycle of constituents, they do not directly expose resource usage, synchronization structure, or global system conditions. By transforming the model into representations such as Petri nets \cite{cortadella1995synthesizing, hei2011automatic}, these aspects can be analysed explicitly, enabling detection of deadlocks, unreachable states, and resource contention. Insights from this analysis can be fed back into the statechart through additional constraints or guarded transitions. This strengthens the link between execution and verification, supporting more reliable SoTS behaviour.
