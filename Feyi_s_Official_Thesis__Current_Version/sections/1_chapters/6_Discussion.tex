\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:discussion}
This chapter summarizes the key takeaways of the thesis. I first examine the utility of the proposed approach (\secref{sec:discussion-utility}), then present directions for future research (\secref{sec:discussion-future}).

\section{On the Utility of the Proposed Approach}\label{sec:discussion-utility}
This section examines the utility of the proposed approach in improving reliability in SoTS by controlling how constituents participate under disturbance.

\subsection{Managing Disturbances in SoTS}
This thesis explores a central conflict in SoS. The dynamic and autonomous nature of SoS allows individual systems to collaborate to achieve more complex behaviour. However, these same properties introduce disturbances that affect their suitability for SoTS. SoTS require reliable and predictable behaviour, which SoS environments do not naturally guarantee. The proposed approach addresses these disturbances by restricting participation based on lifecycle state, shifting disturbance handling responsibility away from individual constituents. In traditional SoS, each system must account for changes in other systems, leading to complex coordination and fault-handling logic. In contrast, the proposed approach defines clear conditions for entry, exit, and contribution through the model. As a result, constituents no longer need to reason about the state of other systems. Instead, they operate based on their own local conditions and permitted interactions. By controlling who can enter the system and how they contribute, the proposed approach reduces disturbances. System evolution---a particularly pressing challenge in complex cyber-physical and digitally twinned systems \cite{david2023towards}---also becomes more predictable, improving the reliability of SoTS.

\subsection{Decoupling Coordination from Implementation Logic}
The proposed approach shifts coordination decisions to the system level, where participation is managed by the lifecycle model. This reduces unnecessary interactions and limits unintended data exposure for constituents \cite{olivero2024systematic}, aligning with principles from the NIST Privacy Framework \cite{enterprise2020nist}. By restricting when and how constituents contribute, the approach enforces controlled interaction and preserves data encapsulation. This also decouples participation logic from implementation logic, clarifying engineering responsibilities. System-level developers define behaviour through the lifecycle model, while service-level developers focus on local functionality. This separation supports modular development and simplifies system evolution.

\subsection{Extensibility of the Model}
Using the proposed approach, I define reliability levels that control how constituents participate to manage disturbances. These levels are not fixed and can be extended by adding new states, transitions, and constraints to the model to capture additional behaviour. For example, systems with stricter requirements may define finer-grained roles or additional conditions for entry and exit. Building on the level~4 model (\secref{sec:lv4}), an additional level could further refine how degraded constituents are handled. A Level~5 extension could refine the health dimension by incorporating temporal constraints, allowing belonging decisions to depend on the stability and duration of a system's condition rather than instantaneous state.

Because the approach is model-driven, these changes can be made directly in the statecharts without modifying the underlying system implementation. Statecharts also provide a clear and accessible representation of constituent behaviour that can be shared across teams. In larger organizations, they can be understood not only by engineers, but also by testing, quality assurance, and system design teams. This enables multiple perspectives to contribute to defining system behaviour while reducing ambiguity during development. The same models can then be translated into system components, ensuring that implemented behaviour matches the specified design.

\section{Future Directions}\label{sec:discussion-future}
This section outlines how the approach can be extended to support more complex behaviour. The model provides a flexible foundation for adding new constraints, adaptive strategies, and formal analysis.

\subsection{Modelling Additional Concerns as Orthogonal Regions}
\secref{sec:two-dimensional-disturbances} introduced a model of SoTS constituents based on belonging and health. While these dimensions support reasoning about reliability, they do not capture all factors that influence whether DTs should contribute to an SoTS. A DT may be operationally healthy and ready to contribute, but still be restricted due to factors such as trust, authorization, confidentiality, or data integrity \cite{kuruppuarachchi2023trusted}. The proposed approach can incorporate these concerns by extending the model with additional orthogonal regions. These regions allow designers to define additional guards and transitions that determine how constituents participate based on a combination of relevant factors. However, adding additional regions increases complexity. Stakeholders must agree on how these concerns are defined and how they affect eligibility to contribute \cite{carney2005topics}. It also requires reasoning about interactions between regions, extending the model beyond reliability to broader dependability concerns \cite{avizienis2004basic}. Statecharts are also an appropriate choice for modelling detailed service interactions~\cite{vanmierlo2018multi}, which positions this approach as a potential general modelling foundation for SoTS services.

\subsection{Dynamic Fault Tolerance in SoTS}
The Level~4 adaptive reliability stage of the model (\secref{sec:lv4}) introduces an additional state called \textit{restricted role}. Constituents enter this state when their health condition worsens. In the architecture described in \secref{sec:demo-compensation}, constituents in this state are supported by a compensation component that predicts missing data to maintain system behaviour. Multiple fault tolerance strategies can be applied in such situations \cite{hanmer2013patterns}. Each strategy introduces trade-offs in resource usage and predictability \cite{narasimhan2002trade-offs}. In dynamic environments, selecting the most appropriate strategy at runtime is more effective than using fixed approaches \cite{ballesteros2022infrastructure}. 

The proposed approach enables this by extending the model with an orthogonal region for strategy selection. Each state in the new region represents a different fault tolerance strategy, and transitions between strategies are triggered by changes in other regions. For example, estimation can handle intermittent missing data, while more severe degradation can trigger data fusion using observations from nearby DTs. Statechart models can be extended with learning-based methods, such as reinforcement learning, to refine guards and transitions based on observed system performance. This allows the approach to improve how fault tolerance strategies are selected over time \cite{dong2023deep}.

\subsection{Formal Verification of SoTS}
The proposed model-based approach supports formal verification of system behaviour under different conditions. Properties such as safety, liveness, and invariants over combinations of belonging and health states can be formally verified \cite{henzinger2023quantitative}. Formal analysis tools can be integrated into the modelling workflow to support automated verification as the system evolves \cite{drusinsky2011modeling}. Further analysis can be supported through model transformations \cite{biehl2010literature}. These transformations provide different views of the system and make additional properties visible \cite{bezivin2006model}. Statecharts capture the lifecycle of constituents but do not directly represent resource usage or global system conditions. These aspects can be analysed by transforming the model into representations such as Petri nets \cite{cortadella1995synthesizing}. The resulting models can then be combined into a single representation, enabling detection of deadlocks, unreachable states, and resource contention. Insights from this analysis can be incorporated back into the statechart as additional constraints or guarded transitions. This strengthens the link between execution and verification and supports more reliable SoTS behaviour.
