\chapter{A Conceptual Model of Disturbances and Reliability in SoTS}\label{ch:reliable-sots}
In SoS, reliability is defined as the ability to maintain operation despite disturbances caused by changes in constituent behaviour~\cite{ferreira2023towards}. These disturbances occur when systems join, leave, or behave incorrectly, and can lead to unreliable SoS—and therefore unreliable SoTS (\secref{sec:disturbances-to-unreliability}). In this chapter, I model disturbances using two dimensions of constituent state: belonging and health (\secref{sec:two-dimensional-disturbances}). I then implement this model using statecharts \cite{harel1987statecharts}, which I use to control how systems participate in the SoTS (\secref{sec:statecharts-reliability}). Building on this, I define reliability levels that place increasing restrictions on system behaviour (\secref{sec:reliability-levels}) and relate them to different SoTS architectural types (\secref{sec:reliability-compatibility}). Finally, I show how this approach reduces reliance on service-level fault tolerance (\secref{sec:reducing-fault-tolerance-burden}).

\section{From Disturbances to (Un)Reliability in SoTS}\label{sec:disturbances-to-unreliability}
SoS differ from traditional engineered systems because their constituents are operationally and managerially independent, and system configurations evolve over time \cite{maier1998architecting}. Constituents may join, leave, or change their behaviour during operation, making it harder to maintain the intended system behaviour. In SoS, disturbances can be defined as structural or operational changes that reduce a system's ability to deliver required capabilities \cite{ferreira2023towards}. These include systems joining or leaving, changes in performance or availability, and faults within individual systems. Because system capabilities depend on coordination between interdependent constituents, such disturbances can propagate across the system.

Similar patterns appear in SoTS. Disturbances are commonly associated with systems becoming unavailable, for example due to intermittent connectivity or dynamic addition and removal of components \cite{parri2019jarvis,human2023design,howard2021greenhouse}. They are also associated with changes in system behaviour, such as missing data, delayed communication, or noisy observations \cite{esterle2021digital,gill2022method,chen2018digital,duan2023digital}. These observations point to two forms of disturbance: a system being unavailable when required, or behaving differently than expected.

Reliability refers to the continuity of correct service over time \cite{avizienis2004basic}. In SoTS, this extends to maintaining operation despite disturbances affecting constituent systems \cite{ferreira2023towards}. Understanding how disturbances arise and propagate is key to reasoning about reliability.

\section{A Two-Dimensional Model of Disturbances in SoTS}
\label{sec:two-dimensional-disturbances}
Disturbances in SoTS can be understood along two dimensions: whether systems participate and whether they operate correctly while participating. Previous SoS work has treated these separately. Lifecycle research focuses on how systems join and leave the SoS \cite{axelsson2019refined,svenson2020situation,hristozov2023modeling}, while reliability research focuses on how SoS behave under faults \cite{ferreira2021reliability, uday2015designing,garro2014reliability,li2025reliability}. In SoTS, these aspects are not independent. How a system operates affects the quality of what it contributes, which in turn affects whether it should continue participating. I therefore represent disturbances using two orthogonal dimensions: \emph{belonging}, which captures whether a system participates in the SoTS and contributes capabilities, and \emph{health}, which captures whether those capabilities are delivered correctly. The \emph{belonging} dimension builds on the idea that constituent systems freely join, remain in, or leave a SoS due to their autonomy \cite{salado2015abandonment}. \citet{axelsson2019refined} frame constituent system states in terms of whether systems belong to and participate in the SoS. This autonomy introduces abandonment, where systems may leave as conditions change \cite{salado2016exile,baldwin2011formation}.

\input{tables/Approach/healthy_belong.tex}

The \emph{health} aspect builds on the observation that disturbances affect a constituent system's ability to deliver its intended capability \cite{ferreira2023towards}. Previous work identifies disturbances arising from factors such as constituent failures and deviations from expected behaviour \cite{ferreira2021reliability, ferreira2024framework}. These disturbances can be decomposed by cause. For example, they may originate internally or externally, and may be accidental or malevolent \cite{ferreira2023towards}. Collectively, they represent conditions that limit a system's ability to operate correctly.

\section{Structured Reasoning about SoTS Reliability Through Statecharts}
\label{sec:statecharts-reliability}
SoTS reliability depends on which systems belong to the SoTS and how they contribute over time. Reasoning about this requires a representation that captures both dimensions and their interaction. State-based modelling provides a way to represent system behaviour and its evolution over time \cite{liang2006comparative}. In this thesis, I propose modeling each constituent's lifecycle using two orthogonal state regions: \emph{belonging} and \emph{health}. This allows each dimension to evolve independently while capturing their interaction. Representing these combinations makes it possible to identify configurations that lead to disturbances. I implement this model using statecharts \cite{harel1987statecharts} to enforce constraints at runtime. Statecharts support orthogonal regions, allowing multiple state dimensions to evolve concurrently, and hierarchy for representing composite states \cite{harel1998modeling}. The model defines constraints through transitions, controlling when constituents can join, remain, or withdraw based on their condition. Enforcing these constraints reduces the likelihood of disturbances and supports more reliable SoTS.

\subsection{State Regions for Belonging and Health}
\label{sec:constituent-state-space}
The model represents each constituent using two orthogonal state regions: \emph{belonging} and \emph{health}. Each region defines a set of states and transitions grounded in existing SoS lifecycle and dependability models.

The belonging region states are based on the SoS lifecycle model from \citet{axelsson2019refined}, shown in \figref{fig:belonging-states} (see \secref{sec:background-sos-states}). A constituent starts in the disengaged state, where it has relevant functionality but is not eligible to participate. Once requirements are met, it moves to prepared, indicating it is ready but not yet part of the system. It can then join in a passive state, where it is recognized but not actively contributing. When it begins contributing, it becomes active.

A constituent starts in the ideal state, where it operates correctly. Issues may introduce defects, leading to internal faults. If faults affect system state or data, the system becomes erroneous. When this affects observable behaviour, it becomes malfunctioning. This may reduce its ability to function correctly, becoming degraded, and eventually lead to failed, where it can no longer operate as intended.

\section{Reliability Levels of SoTS}\label{sec:reliability-levels}
The statechart model defines the possible combinations of belonging and health during system operation. Not all combinations are desirable. Some may disrupt ongoing behaviour or prevent the system from achieving its intended outcomes. Reliability in SoTS is supported by restricting which combinations of states are permitted. This provides a systematic way to express reliability requirements as constraints. By varying how strict these constraints are, different levels of reliability in SoTS can be defined.

\input{tables/Approach/levels.tex}

The following sections present four example levels---\textit{Reactive}, \textit{Proactive}, \textit{Controlled}, and \textit{Adaptive}---to illustrate how the model can be used to define and enforce these constraints. Each level introduces additional restrictions on system behaviour. These levels are illustrative rather than exhaustive. Different applications may require additional states, transitions, or constraints. Table~\ref{tab:reliability-levels} summarizes the levels and how they are enforced.

\subsection{Level 1: Reactive Reliability}

\begin{figure}[htp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/Approach/lv1.pdf}
\caption{Level 1: Reactive Reliability}
\label{fig:lv1}
\end{figure}
Level~1 defines the baseline participation lifecycle for constituents in a SoTS. As noted by \citet{axelsson2019refined}, systems must become aware of each other to participate. This is captured through announcement actions on lifecycle transitions such as \textit{join SoS}, \textit{leave SoS}, \textit{join constellation}, and \textit{leave constellation}, allowing systems to observe changes in the SoTS structure. At this level, no constraints are placed on the relationship between belonging and health. Systems may join, participate, and leave regardless of their condition, reflecting loose coupling in open environments. Disturbances are handled reactively: changes are announced after they occur, but nothing prevents destabilizing situations.


% ------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Level 2: Proactive Reliability}

\begin{figure}[htp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/Approach/lv2.pdf}
\caption{Level 2: Proactive Reliability}
\label{fig:lv2}
\end{figure}

\textit{Guarantees from Level~1 are preserved.} Level~2 introduces additional constraints to prevent disruptive situations from occurring. SoS environments often involve dynamic entry and exit of constituents, which can disrupt ongoing collaborations \cite{axelsson2019refined,sage2007processes,fang2020architectural}. To address this, the \textit{Active} state is refined into three substates---\textit{Pending Entry}, \textit{Participating}, and \textit{Pending Exit}. Timed transitions introduce short stabilization periods, giving the SoTS and other constituents time to adjust before changes take effect. In addition, systems in the \textit{Failed} state are not permitted to join the SoTS, and failure during participation triggers a transition to the \textit{Prepared} state. According to \citet{parhami1994multi-level}, a failed system can no longer operate as intended, so it cannot remain in the SoTS. Disturbances are mitigated by restricting participation of unreliable systems and introducing stabilization periods to prepare for configuration changes.


% ------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Level 3: Controlled Reliability}

\begin{figure}[htp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/Approach/lv3.pdf}
\caption{Level 3: Controlled Reliability}
\label{fig:lv3}
\end{figure}
\textit{Guarantees from Level~2 are preserved.} Level~3 introduces control over how systems enter and leave the SoTS. SoS coordination often involves negotiation between participating systems \cite{axelsson2019refined,fisher2006emergent,damm2015conceptual}. To capture this, the \textit{Passive} state is refined to include \textit{Available} and \textit{Negotiating} substates. A system may only request to join when in the \textit{Ideal} health state, while the SoTS may invite a \textit{Passive} system to become \textit{Active} when additional capabilities are required. Leaving is also controlled. Systems cannot exit freely if they are still relied upon by other systems, using transitions such as \textit{exitDenied} to delay withdrawal when necessary. This model prevents disturbance-causing situations by controlling how systems are admitted and when they are allowed to leave.

\subsection{Level 4: Adaptive Reliability}\label{sec:lv4}

\begin{figure}[htp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/Approach/lv4.pdf}
\caption{Level 4: Adaptive Reliability}
\label{fig:lv4}
\end{figure}
\textit{Guarantees from Level~3 are preserved.} Level~4 adapts behaviour based on system condition. The \textit{Participating} state is refined into \textit{Full Role} and \textit{Restricted Role} substates. When a system is in lower health states, it may continue in a restricted role, where its contribution is limited and, where possible, compensated for to reduce its impact on overall system behaviour. If health degrades further to the \textit{Degraded} state, the system is transitioned to \textit{Pending Exit}. As defined in Level~2, this leads to a controlled transition back to the \textit{Passive} state after the stabilization period, ensuring that removal does not disrupt ongoing activities. Disturbances are mitigated by limiting and compensating for contributions from systems in lower health states, and by removing systems in a controlled manner before failure occurs.



\section{Mapping Reliability Levels to SoTS Types}\label{sec:reliability-compatibility}

\input{tables/Approach/compatibility.tex}

The reliability levels defined in this chapter specify how systems join, operate, and leave the SoTS. Each level is characterized by mechanisms in the statechart model, and its feasibility depends on whether these mechanisms can be enforced at the system level. \citet{ferreira2023towards} observe that the level of authority over constituent systems is a key factor in SoS reliability, with centralized systems better able to manage disturbances than decentralized ones.

\tabref{tab:sots-reliability-compatibility} summarizes how reliability levels map to SoTS types based on whether the required mechanisms can be enforced. In directed SoTS, a central authority enforces all mechanisms, so all levels are feasible. In acknowledged SoTS, a coordinating authority exists but cannot fully enforce behaviour, so mechanisms beyond monitoring rely on cooperation between constituents. This can be achieved through agreements or voting, but is not guaranteed. In collaborative and virtual SoTS, systems act independently without central control. As a result, mechanisms that require enforcement cannot be ensured and depend entirely on voluntary behaviour. As reliability levels require stronger forms of control, they become progressively more difficult to achieve in more complex SoTS.


\section{Fault Tolerance through SoTS Reliability}
\label{sec:reducing-fault-tolerance-burden}
Disturbances in SoTS cannot be fully eliminated due to their complexity and dynamic nature \cite{nielsen2015systems,ferreira2023towards}. As a result, fault-tolerance mechanisms are still required to detect and manage faults during operation \cite{hanmer2013patterns,avizienis2004basic}. In SoTS, constituent systems rely on inputs from other systems. When a constituent suddenly leaves, fails, or behaves incorrectly, these inputs may become missing, delayed, or inconsistent. This directly affects system behaviour and any functionality built on top of these interactions. In practice, fault-tolerance mechanisms are often implemented within these services, requiring them to handle both internal faults and issues caused by external disturbances.

In this chapter, I use the proposed model to manage belonging and health at the architectural level. By enforcing constraints on how systems join, operate, and leave, this approach reduces the number of disturbance scenarios that individual services must handle. Services can then focus on faults within their own functionality. This separation helps clarify responsibilities across layers. The SoTS layer manages system-level behaviour by applying the proposed model to control how systems operate within the SoTS; while individual services handle local fault tolerance. Reliability therefore emerges from a layered combination of architectural control and service-level mechanisms.
