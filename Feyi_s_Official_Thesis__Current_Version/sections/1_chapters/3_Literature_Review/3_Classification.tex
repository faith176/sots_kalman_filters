\section{A Classification Framework for SoTS}\label{sec:literature-review-classification}

To systematically organize how Digital Twins (DTs) and System-of-Systems (SoS) principles are combined, we developed a classification framework grounded in classical SoS theory~\cite{maier1998architecting, maier2014role} and foundational DT concepts~\cite{kritzinger2018digital}. Using a mixed sample–case generalization approach~\cite{wieringa2015six}, each study from the corpus (\secref{sec:literature-review-study-design}) was decomposed into architectural abstractions to reveal recurring organizational patterns.

The metamodel in \figref{fig:metamodel-sots} captures the core entities found in SoTS. A \textit{System} forms the fundamental SoS constituent and can be hierarchically composed. Each system may be represented by one or more \textit{Digital Twins} synchronized with their corresponding \textit{Physical Twins}. DTs follow typical layering structures~\cite{kritzinger2018digital}, while SoS constituents retain \textit{weak coupling} and operational independence. In contrast, DTs maintain \textit{strong coupling} with their physical systems through continuous feedback loops. A \textit{Controller} (often a higher-level DT) coordinates global objectives and decision-making. The color scheme introduced here is used consistently in all subsequent architectural figures.

Six recurring SoTS architectures were identified (\figref{fig:sots-architectures}). Four align with Maier’s canonical SoS types, directed, acknowledged, collaborative, and virtual, while two reflect emerging DT-centric specializations unique to SoTS. Each instance highlights a distinct pattern of authority, autonomy, and coordination among DTs and physical systems. Directed SoTS adopt a strict hierarchy in which a central DT sets global goals and orchestrates subordinate DTs, whereas acknowledged SoTS retain a coordinating DT but allow local DTs to define their own internal goals. Collaborative SoTS remove centralized authority entirely, relying on peer-to-peer choreography in which DTs voluntarily coordinate based on shared interests. Virtual SoTS extend this further, with autonomous DTs forming and dissolving relationships dynamically in response to context and local objectives. In contrast, two additional forms arise from DT-driven specialization. A System of Specialized DTs consists of multiple DTs twinning different analytical or functional perspectives of the same physical system, requiring horizontal coordination to maintain a coherent overall representation. The broader System of Specialized DTs and Specialized Systems generalizes this across multiple heterogeneous systems, where DTs with overlapping scopes coordinate primarily through shared standards rather than hierarchical control. Together, these six architectures capture the dominant structural patterns by which SoTS organize authority, autonomy, and coordination.
