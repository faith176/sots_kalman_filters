\section{A Classification Framework for SoTS}\label{sec:literature-review-classification}

To systematically organize how Digital Twins (DTs) and System-of-Systems (SoS) principles are combined, we developed a classification framework grounded in classical SoS theory~\cite{maier1998architecting, maier2014role} and foundational DT concepts~\cite{kritzinger2018digital}. Using a mixed sample–case generalization approach~\cite{wieringa2015six}, each study from the corpus (\secref{sec:literature-review-study-design}) was decomposed into architectural abstractions to reveal recurring organizational patterns.

The metamodel in \figref{fig:metamodel-sots} captures the core entities found in SoTS. A \textit{System} forms the fundamental SoS constituent and can be hierarchically composed. Each system may be represented by one or more \textit{Digital Twins} synchronized with their corresponding \textit{Physical Twins}. DTs follow typical layering structures~\cite{kritzinger2018digital}, while SoS constituents retain \textit{weak coupling} and operational independence. In contrast, DTs maintain \textit{strong coupling} with their physical systems through continuous feedback loops. A \textit{Controller} (often a higher-level DT) coordinates global objectives and decision-making. The color scheme introduced here is used consistently in all subsequent architectural figures.

\subsection{Architectural Instances}\label{subsec:literature-review-classification-instances}

\input{figures/Literature Review/Combined_Classifications}

Six recurring SoTS architectures were identified (See \figref{fig:sots-architectures}). Four align with Maier’s canonical SoS types, directed, acknowledged, collaborative, and virtual, while two reflect emerging DT-centric specializations unique to SoTS. Each instance highlights a distinct pattern of authority, autonomy, and coordination among DTs and physical systems.

\subsubsection{Directed SoTS}\label{sec:literature-review-classification-instance-directed}

Directed SoTS mirror the strict hierarchy of directed SoS. A central controller, typically a high-level DT, imposes global goals, while subordinate DTs execute tasks under top-down orchestration. This structure favors predictability and uniform coordination.

\subsubsection{Acknowledged SoTS}\label{sec:literature-review-classification-instance-acknowledged}

Acknowledged SoTS retain a central DT but without coercive control. The controller aligns high-level objectives, while local DTs maintain autonomy over internal behavior. Coordination is cooperative and rule-based rather than command-driven.

\subsubsection{Collaborative SoTS}\label{sec:literature-review-classification-instance-collaborative}

Collaborative SoTS remove centralized authority. DTs coordinate through peer-to-peer \textit{choreography}~\cite{peltz2003web}, forming voluntary coalitions to achieve shared objectives. Each DT may provide and consume services dynamically.

\subsubsection{Virtual SoTS}\label{sec:literature-review-classification-instance-virtual}

Virtual SoTS represent fully emergent ecosystems. DTs autonomously form and dissolve relationships based on context, situational awareness, and local goals. Coordination is opportunistic and adaptive, relying on high DT autonomy and cognition.

\subsubsection{System of Specialized DTs}\label{sec:literature-review-classification-instance-spec-dt}

A System of Specialized DTs consists of multiple DTs twinning the same physical system, each focused on a specific analytical or functional domain. Coordination is primarily horizontal among DTs to ensure holistic representation.

\subsubsection{System of Specialized DTs and Specialized Systems}\label{sec:literature-review-classification-instance-spec-dt-and-system}

This general form extends specialization across both digital and physical layers. Multiple DTs twin heterogeneous systems, often with overlapping scopes. Coordination relies on shared data standards rather than hierarchy, enabling interoperability across specialized domains.
